With Book of Hours , Ellen O’Connell Whittet Finds a New Path to Justice

Ellen O’Connell Whittet speaks to Devon about how her Catholic upbringing informed her experiences with pregnancy; fictionalizing her own story; and finding new paths to justice outside of the law.

By Hannah Jackson

September 15, 2026

At the beginning of the pandemic, author Ellen O’Connell Whittet was preparing to publish her first book and give birth to her first child. By the time her memoir, What You Become in Flight, hit shelves that April, Whittet had told her entire life story—including her challenges with an eating disorder and a ballet career cut short—with no idea what lay ahead.

When Whittet experienced an act of obstetric violence during her already arduous labor, she knew that she would one day write about it. But, she realized, the story would perhaps be better told in a work of fiction. The result is Book of Hours, out today from Dzanc Books, in which Whittet weaves together the violence that she experiences at the hands of her doctor with her protagonist’s visions of canonized saints.

“I am very clearly not the person that I’m writing about,” she says. “But [that] I could give the experience to somebody that feels like justice.”

Here, Ellen O’Connell Whittet speaks to Devon about how her Catholic upbringing informed her experiences with pregnancy, what it meant to fictionalize her own story, and finding new paths to justice outside of the law.

This conversation has been edited and condensed.

Devon: When did you know that you were going to write about your birth experience? When did you feel ready to write about it?

Ellen O’Connell Whittet: I think I knew right away. Whenever anything big happens to you when you’re a writer, you’re like, “Well, I’m going to write about this somehow.” A complicating thing is that I was involved in this lawsuit against the OB after the baby was born, so I couldn’t talk about it while that was going on. I was aware that if we settled, there would be an NDA so that I could not talk about it afterwards.

We filed this claim as medical battery and sexual battery because the statute of limitations had already passed for just medical malpractice. We went through about two years of this lawsuit before the judge finally said this was actually medical malpractice. So the lawsuit was dismissed, which always makes me feel a little bit embarrassed. I don’t have that further validation from another institution, from the legal institution, that what I experienced happened or was bad enough to do anything about.

But my lawyer really quickly said, “The law is one avenue for justice, and there are a lot of other avenues for justice that maybe actually would be more satisfying to you. You’re a writer, and now you can write about it.” And when I think about what justice means in a delivery room, I think it means autonomy. It means listening to a patient. It means minimizing things that might feel upsetting or violent while a woman or birthing person is going through an incredibly intense, life-changing, physically altering circumstance that’s really scary. And so, in a way, that feels like justice. I think part of what made me know that I could write about this is that it would be another way to advocate for women.

So much of the book is interwoven with themes of Catholicism and sainthood. You were raised Catholic. How did your upbringing inform your own understanding and experience of pregnancy?

There’s a pretty hard-baked belief in Catholicism that suffering is good. Suffering is a way to feel closer to Christ. That’s a lesson that I internalized in my K-12 Catholic school education—that suffering is noble and suffering is actually to be endured and to be celebrated, in a way. Those saints are saints because they suffered. I mean, all of those women who were canonized were canonized for incredible psychological and physical torture that they endured in order to sometimes stave off having to get married. It goes pretty deep. I think in a way it probably felt like they were making a choice to suffer in order to escape a worse life.

You describe the experience of giving birth as “a saint who accidentally survived her own martyrdom and would keep surviving it the rest of her life.”

I think in American motherhood—when we have so little support from the government, from medical institutions—unless you pay for help, it’s very rare to get it. So it does feel like you just have to martyr yourself over and over and over again. The experience of being pregnant and giving birth also feels like a giving up of yourself. You’re doing it in service of something that hopefully you want to do. But in birth, I remember the phrase that kept coming to me right after it happened was, “I was burned at the stake.” That’s what it felt like—I’m being martyred right now. I was being martyred in service, I guess, of expediency and ease for the physician. Plus, once you have a baby, it’s like you come so secondary to everybody else in the family. Their needs precede your own.

At what point did you realize that Book of Hours wasn’t another memoir, but a work of fiction?

When you’re writing a memoir, it has to be in conversation with current events or with some kind of larger cultural conversation. Fiction feels like you’re allowed to tell a smaller story without making a big political statement. I just realized I can tell a small story in fiction in a way that I didn’t feel like memoir allows for. I think by putting it on somebody else, somebody external to me, I was able to see why one person’s story could stand in for a lot of other people’s experiences—or maybe speak to them.
