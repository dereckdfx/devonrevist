At Lévy Gorvy Dayan, Wangari Mathenge Puts Together the Pieces of a Self

Mathenge has made her Lévy Gorvy Dayan debut with “Wangari Mathenge: Re-membering,” which features five new paintings and the artist’s first sculptures, made collaboratively with her mother and her mother’s friends in Nairobi.

By Stephanie Sporn

September 30, 2026

When her father began training at the Commonwealth Secretariat—the central intergovernmental agency of the Commonwealth—Wangari Mathenge, then just six weeks old, moved with her family from Nairobi to London. Five years later, they returned to Kenya, where she remained for school. Yet Mathenge—now an artist who splits her time between Nairobi and Buckinghamshire, England—never quite felt like she belonged.

“When I step into the British community, they say, ‘You’re a foreigner.’ When I step into the Kenyan community, they say, ‘You’re a mzungu.’ I’m a foreigner everywhere,” she says.

With English as her native tongue—and the language a centerpiece of the post-colonial Kenyan education system—Mathenge eventually learned Swahili, the unifying language among Kenyan tribes. However, unlike her older siblings, she never learned to speak Gikuyu, her parents’ traditional language (and the name of Kenya’s largest ethnic group, to which her family belongs). “One day you wake up and are like, Wait a minute, who am I?” she says.

The question of her own hybrid identity is at the core of Mathenge’s multidisciplinary practice, which primarily consists of monumental paintings with domestic scenes peopled by assured Black figures—often avatars for herself. Whether it’s their pattern-filled compositions or the deeper symbolism behind them, her paintings are clearly resonating: Just seven years after her first solo exhibition at Roberts Projects in Los Angeles, Mathenge has made her Lévy Gorvy Dayan debut in the New York gallery’s marquee September exhibition slot. Open through October 24, “Wangari Mathenge: Re-membering” features five new paintings and the artist’s first sculptures, made collaboratively with her mother and her mother’s friends in Nairobi.

“There is honesty and sophistication in the way Wangari tells a complicated story; asking tough questions about what is history (personal and collective), memory, and the meaning of ‘true identity,’” gallerist Amalia Dayan writes in an email. She goes on to describe a “sense of maturity and fearlessness both in how she approaches color and composition and also how she treats the subject matter and even her journey as an artist.”

Although she recognized her artistic calling in high school, Mathenge pursued law in Washington, D.C., before beginning a successful career as an attorney in Massachusetts. (She recalls her parents “mapping out” her career: “Basically, it was ‘You’re either a doctor, lawyer, or entrepreneur.’”) Over the years that followed, Mathenge felt a “crisis of spirit and non-fulfillment” until she was encouraged to apply for an MFA program. Then, just as she was about to start at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago (where she would graduate in 2021), Mathenge was offered her first solo show at Roberts Projects. “It was a sign for me that I was on the right track,” says the artist. (Still, she keeps her law license active.)

Over the last decade, art has proven a valuable tool for reconciling aspects of Mathenge’s identity—as illustrated by her Re-membering series, which she began in fall 2024. The concept of “re-membering” comes from Kenyan author Ngugi wa Thiong’o’s 2009 book Something Torn and New: An African Renaissance, which also draws on Barbadian poet and writer Kamau Brathwaite’s idea of the “nation language” (the English-influenced language spoken by enslaved people and laborers in the Caribbean). Though these writers primarily discuss the loss and transformation of language due to colonial intervention, Mathenge believes the same can happen to anything creative and cultural, as it pertains to diasporic and continental Africans.

“The idea is that from the fragments of what’s torn, you then create something new,” the artist explains. “In my case, I’m recalling the memory of my ancestors. If you don’t try to re-member—as opposed to dismember—then you’ll forever be lost as a society and as an individual. You will not be able to heal from the wounds of all the things that have caused this dismemberment, and you will not be able to think critically about how you want to push your culture forward.”

As part of her process of recalling, Mathenge embarked on an extensive study of Kenyan colonial literature and cultural objects from 1900 to 1963, the year the country gained its independence from the United Kingdom. “It blew my mind,” Mathenge says, referring to discoveries like the fact that Gikuyus were once put in internment camps. Attempting to understand her own history, Mathenge produced the self-reflective Re-membering series, in which contemplative figures are enveloped in a cacophony of books and objects.

Whereas the original paintings were about recalling, Mathenge says the new Re-membering works on view at Lévy Gorvy Dayan are about taking action. The artist aims for the works to feel timeless, but the influence of the 1960s is clear from the shift dresses and psychedelic wallpapers. In some instances, Mathenge drew on fashions and décor she saw in her family’s old photos. But for the first time in her practice, “there’s a psychic automatism, a sort of surrealism happening,” she explains, likening her process to a writer’s stream of consciousness. Despite their incredibly dense compositions, nothing in the works was pre-sketched. “It felt very natural,” Mathenge says, “but a lot of the painting has objects that I don’t necessarily understand.”

As for the intentional elements, several women are pictured in action. In Re-membering XI (To Leave This Steady Unendurable Land), for example, a woman is pulling the cord of a Roman blind at the fore of the painting. “She is suggesting a refusal of the viewer to intercept what is happening inside that room—it’s a private moment,” says Mathenge, also pointing out “mischievous” motifs of teddy bears and dolls being dismembered. “There’s a lot of refusal of received culture. There is a remapping of territory,” she adds, noting the recurrence of maps and globes in the paintings.

In Re-membering XIV (The Shape of Inheritance), Mathenge gets meta; women can be seen creating the Gikuyu-dress-inspired sculpture Re-membering IX (The Shape of Inheritance) that’s also on view in the gallery. Both that piece, which is displayed on a dress form, and Re-membering VIII (Before Us, After Us, Ever Changing), an abstracted knitted textile composition within a wooden doorframe, were made with Mathenge’s mother and her friends in Nairobi, who are all in their 80s. Though many Kenyan women grew up hand-knitting (they were taught by British missionaries who rejected their traditional animal-hide clothing), it had been decades since most of Mathenge’s circle had used the skill. It proved to be a kind of bonding experience, and the group has since started their own knitting club. “Sometimes you don’t think that you have anything useful to offer people, then the thing you least expect affects you in a profound way,” says Mathenge. She is gratified by having found an organic way to engage her community in her work. “It was challenging, but a lot of fun. I really enjoyed making these sculptures, and I hope it shows.”

The knitted sculptures, which Mathenge informally refers to as “the dress” and “the map,” were conceived in dialogue. “The dress ends up in a landscape, whereas the abstracted map ends as a dress,” says the artist. While the former also references the voluminous Victorian bustle, the latter evokes the traditional box-shaped apron and its rope-like fringes that Gikuyu women used to wear. (Mathenge specifically points to a circa 1906-10 apron, now in the collection of the British Museum, as inspiration.) Both sculptures comment on Western-imposed fashion and changing notions of accepted attire. “You wore your biography on your body,” says Mathenge of the apron, which denoted a distinct stage of womanhood.

It seems Mathenge paints and sculpts her own biography, and that her latest body of work is helping her come to terms with her diasporic history. “I’m in a happy midst of exploring my identity. I’m at the point where I have learned so much that I feel comfortable in then questioning and moving forwards,” Mathange says. “I would not say I’ve reached a resolution because I think discovering will be endless.”
