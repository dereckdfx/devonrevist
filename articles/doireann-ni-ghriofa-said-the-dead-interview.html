Poet Doireann Ní Ghríofa on Her Searching New Book, Said the Dead

In “Said the Dead,” we meet and follow extraordinary women with extraordinary lives, in a time when Europe and Ireland are in the midst of World War I. As infectious disease outbreaks tear through a women’s asylum in Cork City, Lucia Strangman, one of Europe’s first female doctors, rises to these challenges and then some.

By Robert Sullivan

September 26, 2026

Ghost in the Throat, the Irish poet Doireann Ní Ghríofa’s wondrous first book, draws on her obsession with an 18th-century poet who was lost to the past despite having written, in 1774, one of the most celebrated remembrances in poetry, The Lament for Art O’Leary. Published in 2020, that book was a collage of remembrance and research, culminating in a new translation of Eibhlín Dubh Ní Chonaill’s lament for her murdered husband. (It began with a declaration: “This is a female text.”)

Said the Dead, Ní Ghríofa’s new book, starts with a small confusion in her public library’s archives—a book slip is sent to an old address. A realtor phones to complain, a call that eventually brings Ní Ghríofa, “on a whim,” to wander Cork Mental Hospital, what was, on opening in the 1850s, one of the largest asylums in Europe. By 2020, however, it had been reduced to a century-and-a-half-old ruin running a mile along the River Lee and in the midst of condo conversion.

She then makes a trip to her city archives, in Cork, where she begins to read the casebooks of the asylum’s female patients—texts about females—which are sad and beautiful and hard to read, though often heartening in ways that possessed Ní Ghríofa and moved her to read on through years and years of doctors’ notations. “She pressed onwards through the crowd,” the narrator writes, “reading each person she met in the particularity of their sorrows, their enmities, their own cherished rage. If she recognized their distress, it was because she, too, had known the lure of the river’s dark silks and the horror of being dragged back from her ending, but even when she recognized wisps of herself in their lives, she understood that her pain was hers, and their pain was theirs.”

In Said the Dead, we meet and follow extraordinary women with extraordinary lives, in a time when Europe and Ireland are in the midst of World War I. Following the Irish uprising against England in 1917, the British retaliation sees Winston Churchill dispatch a brutal police force to Ireland that attacks civilians and ends with Cork City burned. There are infectious disease outbreaks that tear through the women’s asylum, pre-antibiotics and vaccines, but there is also Lucia Strangman, one of Europe’s first female doctors, who rises to these challenges and then some.

If it all sounds harrowing, that’s because it is, but the poetry of the book leads us forward in ways that remind us how grief is not so much something that individuals or even cities resolve, but that we carry forward, allowing us to feel life more deeply. In that spirit, the myriad voices the reader encounters aren’t so much confused at the close of Said the Dead as overlapping, like waves in the sea, and we maybe feel the urge to write ourselves a better life, and in so doing, direct others. “If you take the next step,” one voice says, “you leave here and make a path so others can come—others who need help—but they will only find me if you write them a map.”

On the week of the publication of the American edition of Said the Dead, Ní Ghríofa spoke to Devon from her home in Cork.

Devon: How did your work on this book begin?

Doireann Ní Ghríofa: I was always very interested in this ruin that I would often pass in our city—the ruin of the old city asylum, and I think like a lot of people I was particularly curious about it because I have often struggled with my own mental health. I had the sense that perhaps in times gone by, people suffering similar distresses to my own would have been within that building looking out. And when I heard that the old casebooks from that hospital had been saved towards the end of the hospital’s existence and donated to the city archive, I was surprised to learn that those casebooks were open to the public and that I could just make an appointment and go to visit them and just read.

So you began to just read?

I have very few talents in life, but one of them is reading, and I sometimes feel that I’m never more myself than when I’m reading and allowed to temporarily inhabit someone else’s sense of being in the world—which I know is kind of a contradiction, but somehow that’s a welcome sensation for me. So when I found myself in the archive, these old casebooks led me to an experience of reading which was different from anything I’d ever experienced in my life. There was a sense of real people on the other side of the page, real human beings’ real lives unfolding as I read, and the stakes felt so high every time I turned the page because I really didn’t know what was going to become of this person.

You were discovering the asylum’s patients when the COVID lockdown began, a difficult time for so many people—how did the pandemic affect your reading?

My first day in the archive visiting these casebooks was at the end of 2020, and it really was the act of reading that led me through the entirety of those years. Reading the casebooks, reading in other archives, but also a sense of reading the cityscape, seeking traces of these lives that had been and gone and yet felt extremely close to me.

The book describes trips to the archive, and then off into the ruins of the asylum, which were crumbling and also being turned into condominiums. And while you report on wondrous discoveries in the disused building and on its fringes—an orchard, strange tunnels, and a hole in a piece of plywood that made for an amazing-sounding accidental camera obscura?-sounding accidental camera obscura—you were also often alone, or worse, coming upon strangers in dark, empty places.

My heart was often beating very fast because the book often put me in situations where I would feel that I had two choices. One was to make the decision, for my own safety and well-being and just my ordinary way of being, to return home and do something safe. The other option was to take a brave choice, which would make for a better book, right? And often I went with the latter.

But in the relative safety of the archives, you also explored?

The moment I would be handed one of the casebooks, I felt, I suppose, what you could only describe as a kind of time travel, whereby this object that I was opening, this book, was immediately able to transport me, through the act of reading, back more than a hundred years to see this young doctor, Dr. Lucia Strangman, as she walked up the hill toward her first job in this vast institution and then began to write, in these same casebooks that I was reading, about each of the women that were arriving at the institution. And her patients and the attention that this young doctor brought to her patients was so compelling to read despite the amount of time that had passed between us.

Her casebook entries, as you cite them, feel like miniature portraits.

She would write things about, you know, their hair, their eyes, their scars, and what was most moving of all to me was often when she and the other doctors would transcribe brief snatches of their speech. It felt almost as though the doctor who was holding the pen and doing the writing was disappearing, like a hologram or something. I could see through the doctor to the real person who was speaking on the other side, and that was an incredibly powerful experience because I felt such kinship with these women who had also inhabited the same places and walked the same streets in the same city that I was walking, and they felt so real and so true.

Lucia is an amazing doctor, or so it appears; she was among the first women to graduate from the Royal College of Surgeons in Ireland. Sure, she comes off as human, in that she misses things, but also as expansive in her thinking, and in her capacity for care, working at a time not only when ideas about mental health are changing but during wars and civic uprisings. It feels as if Lucia was treating the casualties.

She really does. And I found that incredibly moving because the book loosely follows Lucia’s working life through the days when she arrives as a young doctor to this vast institution—and really devotes herself to the care of her patients there—and then as her career progresses, she turns more toward what felt to me like almost a radical hope, a hope that she and her husband, who was also a doctor, could effect real change in people’s lives by adopting new methods, by opening a new clinic from within their home, where they seem to be practicing a very early form of talk therapy. I mean, it’s extraordinary to imagine that in the 1910s and 1920s in a city in the south of Ireland, that someone suffering an early form of mental distress would be invited to their home to, you know, lie on the sofa, to talk. And they brought different techniques, of mesmerism and hypnosis, to bear on their work with these patients. And they found a lot of success in this clinic. So you have that sense of transition, from the vast institution towards a more person-centered path toward healing.

Was it her husband, John, who suggested they might help people before they were committed to an asylum?

Yes, that was the sense of it, and they really had the feeling that their approach was so successful that this could be an opportunity to eradicate mental illness in its entirety if they could only manage to meet people at an earlier point in their mental distress and help them. And I just—I found that extraordinary.

The book begins with a reference to an eviction, a landlord telling your family to leave. Is it just me, or is this book partly about how hard it is to live in cities and also the great joy that comes from living in cities, to fall into other people’s paths as people move through spaces together, as we move in the past that is also alive?

I came to Cork City when I was 17, from a very rural place in the west of Ireland, County Clare, a really beautiful, quite stark landscape, a place of small farms, of very narrow roads. Cork City, by American standards, is a very small city, but through the eyes of a teenager coming from County Clare, it was vast and so alive, and I never lost that sense of wonder of being in the city, of just being present in a city, of being in a body moving through a city and the sense that when we’re in the city, yes, we’re in the city, but the city is also in us. And I think that that’s part of what was at play with Said the Dead—the sense of making a study of that sensation. That the city is in us, but it’s not just the current iteration of the city. It’s all the many versions of our own selves that we’re passing when we’re walking the same streets, that I passed the 17-year-old version of me who walked the same street. But now when I encounter and move through the city, I’m also feeling the sense of my own shadow kind of puddling through the shadows of all the women who were held in this institution, but whose lives before and often after their time in the institution were crisscrossing this city and the city streets too. And so it has really deepened my understanding of what it is that a city is. Yes, it’s the intersection of many, many, many lives in this moment, but it’s also many lives over time, and the fact that we’re all crisscrossing each other’s paths. That just blows my mind, really.

What surprised you about your encounter with the patient histories?

Part of what I maybe wasn’t expecting when I began to study the history of this asylum was just how much of the human histories I would find unfolding there were histories of recovery. I was expecting to find primarily histories of cruelty, and there were very, very sad life stories that I encountered there, but there were also many stories of recovery. And there was the element of mental distress that we rarely see articulated, which is, okay, when things start to alleviate, how does a person go about putting their life together again and returning to themselves? And that was an element that I thought about a lot, because with every person who recovered and left the asylum, they were also leaving my grasp. And they were leaving the casebooks, and I could no longer see transcriptions of their ordinary speech. They were returning to the privacy of their own lives. And so I was really fascinated by that, that they were shaking off our eyes and returning to their own lives and returning to themselves, and that there was a sense of grasping the pen or the pencil from the doctor’s hand or from the writer’s hand and beginning to just write their own story again. But that it’s a story that we don’t get to read. It’s theirs, it’s not ours to read.

You’ve made the point that you had to learn to use the tools of historians, but at the same time, I wonder if your tools as a poet helped in seeing the patient’s new beginnings?

One thing I love about studying the past is the sense that, I may have imagined this or made this up in my head, but the idea that people will be going around with a big ring full of keys. I feel I try to go about my days gathering different keys but not knowing what kind of doors they’ll unlock, and with Said the Dead, I didn’t know what I was going to find but I kept trying all these different keys I had against all the obstacles and locked doors I met, and sometimes it was the logic of poetry that led me through an obstacle to imagine my way through to the other side. So, yes, it’s not a straight history by any manner or means, this book. It comes at its questions from strange and oblique angles, because it’s exactly what you said—they’re the keys that I have. They’re the keys that I’ve picked up over my years and put on my ring. And they aren’t the same kinds of keys that a qualified historian would have.

In the spirit of the creative leap that this project is, I’m not going to ask you about genre—specifically, what genre you’d say this book is.

A bold move!
