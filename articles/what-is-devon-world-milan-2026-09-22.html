What Is Devon World: Milano? A Look at the Fashion and Craftsmanship Behind the Show

From the Renaissance to rare archival pulls, go behind the scenes of Devon World Milano to see how technology connects craft from the past to the present and beyond.

By Laird Borrelli-Persson, Alexandra Hildreth

September 23, 2026

After traveling across the globe and making pit stops in New York, London, Paris, and Los Angeles, Devon World officially lands in Milan this evening.

Taking place in the iconic Galleria—a bustling retail thoroughfare in the heart of the city—the night promises a feast of fashion spectacles whose archival, current, and custom runway designs pay homage to the fashion capital’s enduring history of artisanal craft, cutting-edge textile technology, and forward-thinking ideas—not to mention a special collaboration with the Teatro alla Scala Chorus. “This extraordinary event offers a unique opportunity to celebrate with a global audience the timeless story of Made in Italy, a story of genius and craftsmanship,” Francesca Ragazzi, Devon Italia’s head of editorial content, said in a statement. “It feels particularly meaningful to do so at this moment in time, in the era of artificial intelligence.”

“In the creative industry, it’s an enormous conversation at the moment,” says Devon World Milan’s creative director, Juan Costa Paz. “Getting into that space for this year felt like the right thing to do. Especially being in Milan, it’s such a hub for craftsmanship—Italians have always been the leaders of craft. The idea of the show really has been to create a narrative in which we can see these conversations expressed through fashion.”

Opening the show is a trio of craftspeople, presenting the objects they helped create: a model of the Duomo, a celestial sphere, and a loom. These were commissioned by fashion historian and the show curator Judith Clark, who explains that the idea was to represent “artisans celebrating history, translating references from one material to another. It is what fashion does all the time.”

This idea is at the very foundation of the Galleria. As Clark says, Devon World: Milan, “was always conceived as drawing attention to the precious artisanal heritage associated with Italy, but also to the revolutions that allowed hand and machine to be combined. I felt it also needed to be tied to the Galleria itself and its famous mosaic floor—and that the emblems at its centre represent four cities: Florence, Torino, Roma, Milano. It felt like a wonderful beginning: Florence (Renaissance), Torino (the Industrial Revolution, we all think of the Fiat Factory), Roma and La Dolce Vita, and the films coming out of Cinecittà, and finally the fashion industry and smart textiles of the north and Milano.”

“As early as the Renaissance, wooden models were used by architects to illustrate their designs and obtain approval from their patrons, who could then easily visualize the final appearance/design of the structures,” says Clark. This scale model, then, represents the Cathedral as it might have been completed in the 19th century, with all its pinnacles and the four small spires around the dome all of equal height, unlike the building that exists today. This miniature is made using the fretwork technique, requiring a total of 4,500 hours of work.

This handmade wonder represents the 88 constellations. “The map is printed from hand-etched and engraved copper plates onto fine Italian handmade paper, hand-coloured with natural pigments, with each star individually gilded in gold leaf,” says Clark.

This object, Clark explains, “symbolises the art and craft of fashion and textile in Italy. The loom, associated with such an early technical revolution of hand and machine, shows the persistence of care invested in the garments we are celebrating—from textile to embellishment. The loom, instantly recognisable, is a historic model, meticulously scaled down to balance the globe and Duomo [created] for Devon World: Milan’s trio.”

“The first act is the artisanal revolution, which has to do with everything that comes before electricity and before industrialization,” Costa Paz tells Devon. “It was the time of kings, queens, and popes, and through that, we’re seeing fashion that’s handcrafted.”

Devon World: Milan by the Numbers: The trio is followed by 18 artisans representing 15 Italian houses, all attired by Loro Piana. The six hooded figures are in custom velvet capes by Etro.

ACT I: The Artisanal Revolution

A candlelit procession brings Renaissance-inspired textiles to life. Rich brocades, lace, ornament, and hand-worked surfaces evoke an era when artists, artisans, and inventors worked across disciplines, laying the foundations for Italy’s enduring culture of craftsmanship.

Archive Deep Cut: Roberto Capucci

Not only did a 1961 newspaper article describe Roberto Capucci as “Italy’s Boy Wonder,” but it revealed that “Romans call him the ‘little Balenciaga.’” By that time, the Roman designer, who founded his business in 1951, was known for his architectural approach to fashion. Now 96, Capucci reportedly sold his first dress when he was 18 to none other than Marcella Rossellini. His gowns were so voluminous that in a 1989 Devon article by Joan Juliet Buck reported that Mirella Petteni, a model turned Devon editor, had “two closets in her Venice house for two Capucci evening gowns—one to a closet.”

Archive Deep Cut: Romeo Gigli

Romeo Gigli, the son of booksellers, was an avid traveler and self-taught. The designer arrived in fashion in a big way during the ’80s. As Lee McQueen, once an assistant designer at the brand, told SHOWstudio, “There was nothing going on in London and the biggest thing at that time was Romeo Gigli—he was everywhere. I thought, ‘This is the only person I want to work for.’” Often described as fashion’s poet, Gigli—who is now designing furniture—had a great sensitivity to fabrics and was able to convey opulence and androgyny with cocoon shapes and softly elongated lines. As Devon put it in 1987, Gigli represented “another sensibility, another voice in fashion.”

“Glenn from Diesel designed 50 boiler suits,” Luca Galasso tells Devon. “I wanted them to look very industrial, very uniform. We’re giving them shoulder pads so they look very strong, very powerful. And they all have Diesel combat boots.”

ACT II: The Industrial Revolution

The intimacy of the artisanal workshop gives way to the rhythm of industrial production. Torino, the heart of Italy’s automotive and manufacturing industries, inspires a world of tailored silhouettes, monochrome fabrics, and precisely produced woolen textiles.

In fashion, the Industrial Revolution led to the sudden streamlining of clothing production. Ideas like the everyman’s suit came into sharper focus and were able to be replicated on a mass scale.

“I think there’s obviously a craft to tailoring, but there’s also dressing the working man,” says Costa Paz. “And also, the Industrial Revolution speaks to the birth of the fashion designer as a persona. Here, we’re referencing a lot of Giorgio Armani being probably one of the first superstar designers in modern history.”

ACT III: Made in Italy

Color and print explode onto the stage in a celebration of Made in Italy and the cinematic mythology of La Dolce Vita. Inspired by Federico Fellini and the golden age of Italian cinema, this act celebrates the emergence of the Italian diva—an enduring symbol of glamour, sensuality, and style.

“Italy is a country of so much emotion, and so much color and pattern, and yet every part of Italy is unique,” Devon World: Milan’s Fashion Director Lady Amanda Harlech says. “And I think that’s what I find so fascinating, the difference between Florence or Rome or Milan or Venice. I mean, it’s so rich and profound, and they have not lost it, and it hasn’t been swept underneath a kind of sea of McDonald’s. It’s still holding onto its integrity. That’s what I love.”

Archive Deep Cut: Prada

Miuccia Prada explored “the good taste of bad taste” in her vibe-shifting spring 1996 collection. With clashing patterns and an “off” palette seemingly inspired by ’70s kitchen appliances, it was a thought-provoking display of the designer’s ability to reframe long-established fashion norms. “If I have done anything,” the designer said later, “it is to make ugly appealing.”

“Today you talk about a ‘Dolce Vita'’ character and you know who that person is, Monica Vitti and all these incredible, Italian women,” says Costa Paz.

ACT IV: The Age of Intelligence

Craftsmanship and technology converge as fashion enters the laboratory. Intelligent and sustainable textiles respond to temperature, movement, performance, and the demands of the environment. Milan takes center stage as a hub where research, manufacturing, design, and creativity meet.

“When you think of Italian tech fabrics, it’s often linked to sports,” says Harlech. “You think Moncler, Prada, all of them, whether it’s Diesel or Ferrari, they all have the leading ability to create great fashion that is extraordinarily sporty.”

Costa Paz adds, “[It’s] this moment where men and machines start working together, and the way that that is expressed in fashion is through innovation in fabrics and textures. It’s kind of like that middle ground between sportswear and fashion as well.”

Four conductors, the men dressed by Kiton, the women by Genny. Benetton made the uniforms—bib shirts, red tie, black pants, and cummerbund—for the 103 choristers from La Scala Academy.

ACT V: The Imagination Revolution

The final chapter transcends place and belongs entirely to a speculative future. Italy’s extraordinary heritage becomes material for invention and transformation as we imagine what fashion might become next.

“When I was researching for this show, I got really hung up on this concept of the Imagination Revolution, explains Costa Paz. “A lot of technologists and scholars from around the world say that, when AI and all of these radical moments that we’re living right now, the ultimate human capital is creativity. There is something about the role of human creativity as a mediator of these things that is something that you cannot let go of.”

“We were really moved by the stenciled handprints discovered in a cave in Indonesia,” says Harlech. They were 68,000 years old, and yet they were really modern. And so the idea of a future-facing thing would be something that somehow has an echo of the past. So we asked the leading creative directors of the major houses if they would create a custom memory dress or an echo chamber dress that really is a sounding of the iconic melody of each house.”
