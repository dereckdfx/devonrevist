John Wilson Is Still Shocked That The History of Concrete Got Made

Wilson’s discursive new film is out now from Magnolia Pictures.

By Alex Jhamb Burns

September 19, 2026

While filmmaker John Wilson was touring his latest movie, The History of Concrete, around Europe, he was gifted a large slab of the material.

“I had to fly from Barcelona to Warsaw with a ton of concrete in my bag that I thought was basically contraband because it was sidewalk tiles that people literally ripped out of the ground [in Barcelona] to give to me,” he tells Devon over Zoom. “Thankfully, they didn’t weigh my bag at all on the Polish airline.”

This wasn’t an isolated incident; other concrete gifts that Wilson has accumulated this year now decorate the backyard in Ridgewood, Queens. Others function as ornaments on his coffee table. “I don’t want to discourage anyone from giving me more concrete, but just try to be mindful about how far away I am from home, potentially,” Wilson quips. (Perhaps his micro-movie theater Low Cinema in Ridgewood would be the best spot to deliver him such a dense bounty.)

So why concrete, and why a feature-length film (Wilson’s first) about it? It’s a long story. “I accidentally made a movie, in certain ways,” Wilson says.

When the foundation of his house begins to crack and the basement floods, Wilson becomes fascinated with the ubiquity of concrete in our modern lives. “It’s embarrassing to know so little about something that occupies so much of your visual environment,” he narrates while the camera pans into a freshly paved sidewalk. Thus begins an odyssey that will take Wilson to Rome, Astoria, a 3,100-mile race in Queens, a Las Vegas concrete convention, a rock concert, and back, all in the effort to secure funding to make a movie about concrete, guided by the principles of the Hallmark class.

Though the pace of production was different, The History of Concrete can be viewed as an extension of the How To series, in which Wilson explored everything from the mundane process of putting up scaffolding to “How to Be Spontaneous.” What unites these seemingly disparate topics is Wilson’s knack for putting himself into scenarios with unusual people that lead him into unexpected territory—a lesson in the virtues of simple observation.

When Wilson is working on a project, he imagines himself amassing material for a collage, or completing a pattern. “I always have these mini games that I’m playing in my head,” he explains, “because I'm guilty of this too. I passively consume a lot of stuff on my phone and it is an active decision you have to make to replace your phone with another object, whether it’s a pencil or a dedicated camera.”

Though Wilson’s face is generally not visible in the frame, his voice and scenes from his life are present throughout the film, weaving together his interviews with a rotating cast of characters. “I’m constantly doxing myself,” Wilson jokes. But he likes to approach his work as though he’s just making it for himself, and its intimate, homegrown quality is ultimately central to its appeal. “Even if you’re just showing something really banal within your house, I think that’s really rare and shocking imagery to certain people because it’s underrepresented in a certain way,” he says. “We've gone so far in the other direction that I think people might crave something a bit more human.”

Despite his general aversion to doing press—“People just seem to want your skin, in a way”—he understands the need to play the marketing game. Early in the film, Wilson goes to the actor Michael Imperioli for advice on getting a project financed. His recommendation is simply to make the thing as inexpensively as possible. (At a press screening for The History of Concrete at Low Cinema a few months ago, Wilson introduced the film by half-joking that he’d expressly created the theater to have somewhere to show it in case it was never acquired.) Now that the movie is complete—and being distributed by Magnolia Pictures—Wilson applauds Imperioli’s wisdom. “You should try not to consider gatekeepers that have this specific idea about what will sell,” he says. “When someone doesn’t want something that I’m making, that is the hottest fire that I can put under myself.”

The History of Concrete is in theaters now.
