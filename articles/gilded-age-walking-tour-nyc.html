7 Fascinating Facts I Learned From an NYC Gilded Age Walking Tour

Keith Taillon takes a Devon writer on a Gilded Age tour of Manhattan, stopping at iconic buildings and streets that tell the history of this iconic era.

By Nicole Kliest

October 7, 2026

On November 1, season four of The Gilded Age will premiere, and all over again, audiences will daydream about residing in ornate Beaux-Arts mansions along Fifth Avenue. While several structures from this era still stand and are open to the public—including the Frick, Cooper Hewitt, and the Morgan Library—there’s something about tracing this era of Manhattan on foot, neighborhood by neighborhood, past rows and rows of private homes, that really feels like traveling back in time. On a recent afternoon, I met up with historian Keith Taillon to do just this: view the city with fresh eyes, but through a nostalgic lens.

In addition to being a historian, author, and tour guide, Keith also runs a popular Instagram account (@keithyorkcity), where he rabbit holes on topics like the demolition of the original Penn Station, why we call it ‘The Big Apple,’ and Gilded Age fun facts galore. He holds a bachelor’s degree in history and a master’s in urban planning, so his tours contextualize the city’s past through its “structural configuration,” as he puts it.

We begin our Gilded Age walkabout on a tree-lined street in Greenwich Village on a sunny Wednesday in late September. Very few tour guides focus on Lower Manhattan when covering the Gilded Age, but for Keith, it’s the start of his entire narrative arc (and for good reason).

Eager to learn why? Continue below for a few of the riveting (and sometimes juicy) tidbits I learned along the way.

The Upper East Is Where the Gilded Age Went to Die

When we think about the Gilded Age (and see its glamorous depictions in film and television), the Upper East Side is often the centerpiece. But this is only half the story. “Most people focus on that neighborhood because it’s where the surviving mansions are,” Keith explains. But when he does his Upper East Side tour, he always tells guests it’s where the Gilded Age went to die. “It’s where the younger generations of families who got their start down here, in houses like these, moved with their inheritance and spent themselves into oblivion just in time for World War I and the Great Depression.” So while the Upper East Side is an example of the excess that led to the end of the Gilded Age, the houses in Greenwich Village up to about 42nd Street are more indicative of the kinds of families who led to the start of the Gilded Age. “I often tell people, 90% of the buildings I’m talking about on my tours Downtown are gone,” he says. “But down here, you’re actually walking in the footsteps of the people that defined how the Gilded Age played out.”

The Nickname ‘Gilded Age’ Is a Clever Mock

It might sound glamorous, but the era’s label is actually a mocking nickname coined by Mark Twain. “He based it off of an era during ancient Greece,” Keith says, explaining it was based on the Gilded Age of Pericles. “Basically, it was this idea that a very, very small wealthy population began to rot the democratic ideals of Greece by becoming overly wealthy and powerful.” Mark Twain borrowed the phrase and wrote a book in 1873 called The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today that poked fun at the fact that post-Civil War there was a very thin veneer of wealth and prosperity. “I always remind people that when you’re talking about the Gilded Age, all the stuff you learn about at the Tenement Museum was all happening at the same time as this,” Keith explains. While the 1% were living in elegant homes in Greenwich Village, Madison Square, Gramercy Park, and Murray Hill, the vast majority were living in Downtown tenements. “That’s why I start my Gilded Age tours Downtown.”

(Note: Keith offers three tours. One that starts Downtown and looks at the Gilded Age’s origins; another that goes through Midtown and talks about the hinge point where old and new money were living side by side and clashing; and the last in the Upper East Side, where the era came to die.)

Mrs. Astor’s Rule to Be in Good Society

Generally, historians agree that the Gilded Age was from the end of the Civil War to World War I, so around the 1860s to 19-teens. And there were economic implications to that. “It began largely because of the industrial push that fueled the war effort in the north, to cities like New York, Baltimore, and Boston,” Keith says. These cities industrialized rapidly, which created a powerful new population of people who had gotten rich. “Not off of generational wealth, but instead off their own ingenuity, their own industrial creations, kind of a bootstrap thing.” Of course, this became one of the great separations. “There’s a great quote attributed to Mrs. Astor—who I doubt actually said this—but it’s that ‘to be in good society, one must be at least three generations separated from the dirty business of making money.” And so became the dividing line between old money and new money.

Grace Church Was a Gilded Age Epicenter

Take a stroll to Grace Church on Broadway and you’ll see several old money names adorning the wall outside the entrance—Schermerhorn, Astor, Livingston. “These are the families who dictated how the city functioned after the Civil War, and it makes sense their names would be all over this church because it was built in 1846,” Keith explains. It’s also where Mrs. Astor was reportedly married, and where Ward McAllister (played by Nathan Lane in the show) had his funeral. “Just generally speaking, Grace Church is an excellent example of a surviving piece of Gilded Age history, but again, it’s much further downtown and is much older than most people appreciate,” Keith says. “That’s another thing I like to remind people: Gilded Age buildings don't necessarily have to have been built during the Gilded Age.”

Evelyn Nesbit and the Murder of Stanford White

Along the route, Keith points out a massive colorful mural. “That’s Evelyn Nesbit,” he says. A leading actress and model during the Gilded Age, on whom a character in Ragtime is based, Nesbit grew tired of modeling and at age 16 asked her mother if she could dance on Broadway. Her mother permitted it, so long as she continued to model during the day and danced at night. During these nighttime performances, she caught the eye of architect Stanford White. “He was married, had a son, and lived on Gramercy Park,” Keith says, “but also kept a couple of love nests around Madison Square.” They began an affair, but she eventually married a young Pittsburgh coal heir named Harry Kendall Thaw—a jealous man with an infamous temper. “He took out a lifelong vendetta against Stanford White,” Keith says, and the day before the couple were taking a ship to Europe, they came to see a show on the roof of Madison Square Garden—a building designed by Stanford White, where he kept an apartment inside the bell tower. White came down to see the show that night as well. “When Harry saw him, he marched over in the middle of a packed audience, right in front of the stage, and shot him three times in the head, killing him on the roof of Madison Square Garden and creating one of the greatest scandals of the Gilded Age.”

The Widening of the Streets Signaled a Shift

A later Gilded Age issue that Keith says doesn’t get a lot of attention is the widening of the streets. “Starting in 1911, the city ordered widening for most major streets, including Fifth Avenue and Madison Avenue, and think about what happens,” he says. All the mansions were forced to accommodate them. “The city, through eminent domain, takes all of their stoops, takes their bay windows, anything that’s jutting out into public space has to be shaved off.” This was, of course, thanks to the automobile. The wealthy began to abandon their mansions because the small buffer between the house and the sidewalk was coinciding with the rise of tabloid journalism. “They lost what separated them from the outside world, and you see a slow recognition that maybe we don’t want to live like this,” he says. “So maybe they moved to the Upper East Side or out of the city entirely to the relative privacy of Florida, California, or to a country estate in the Berkshires or Long Island.”

The Dispute Between Old and New Money Actually Played Out in Murray Hill

For our final stop, we pause in front of the Morgan Library, across the street from the Beaux-Arts Joseph Raphael De Lamar mansion (now the Polish Consulate). “One of my greatest beefs with the show,” Keith says, with a laugh, “is this kind of dispute between old money and new money really played out down here, not up there.” Meaning, no old money families built houses like the stoic, fictional van Rhijn house uptown. “When old money did move up there, they adopted the new money style.” For example, Mrs. Astor’s uptown home was a lavish French Renaissance double mansion—a stark contrast to her previous, understated brownstone at the corner of Fifth Avenue and 34th Street in Murray Hill (where the Empire State Building now stands). After decades of holding the fort, the old money sensibilities eventually gave way to new. “It signaled that the fight for keeping the old aesthetic was over. By building a house like this, she gave the green light to all who were following her to the Upper East Side that it was okay to build like this.”
