Past and Future Collide in Arlene Shechet’s Otherworldly Sculptures

“Arlene Shechet: She Said She Said” is on view at Pace Gallery’s 508 and 510 West 25th Street locations through October 24, 2026.

By Grace Edquist

September 22, 2026

It’s a picturesque June day, and Arlene Shechet is showing me around her studio in Kingston, about two hours north of New York City. “Here’s our little family of chainsaws,” she says. It’s a delightfully unexpected place to start the tour, fitting for an artist whose recombinant objects of ceramic, wood, and metal have earned her a reputation as one of our most innovative living sculptors.

The capabilities of the 5,000-square-foot space are impressively varied. There’s just about every tool you can think of, plus a metal shop, a custom wood-drying kiln, a computer- and paper-modeling zone, shelves stacked with bins of clay, and a serene courtyard surrounded by concrete walls. “My own little LA in Kingston,” she jokes of the latter.

Shechet, 75, has had a studio in Kingston for 10 years, four of them in this particular space. It’s where she makes the big stuff. She still keeps a studio in Woodstock, where she’s lived for more than two decades. But she (or rather, her work) had outgrown it. “It’s up a dirt road, very hard to move sculpture, which I thought was the right idea to keep me under control,” she says, breaking into a sly grin. “But I lost control.”

A good thing, in this case. Shechet’s 2024 show “Girl Group” at Storm King, which brought six soaring sculptures to the grounds of the Hudson Valley art park, was lauded as a triumph. (One assessment in The New York Review: “As a collagist and an amalgamator, as a colorist, as a shape generator, and as a no-nonsense feminist heroine, Shechet has few equals.”) It was her first time making outdoor sculptures at such heroic scale, but for Shechet—at that point best-known for having elevated ceramic work into the arena of fine art—it seemed like a natural extension of a practice that has always been rooted in boundary-pushing exploration.

This fall, Shechet presents her follow-up to Storm King with “She Said She Said,” now on view at Pace Gallery in Chelsea. For the exhibition, Shechet has made three new monumental sculptures—two in the colorful, whimsical vein of “Girl Group” and one in Corten, her first time using the weather-resistant steel—plus a suite of otherworldly ceramic installations.

“Each piece has many years behind it where I let it go, then I come back to it, then I let it go again,” Shechet says. Such unhurried consideration yields sculptures whose every viewpoint contains a surprise. You can’t know the entire story from just one angle.

Take Eve, the first piece you’ll encounter when entering the front gallery space. At 16 feet high, it’s the tallest of the new works (measured to the inch to fit in the room) and painted in pale shades of green and gray-blue, “like the beginning of things,” Shechet says. At the very top of one side there’s a small squiggly cutout in the aluminum, a reward for those who look all the way up. At the base, a silver blob that resembles a squished lychee holds up a swoop of terraced metal. The whole thing reminds me of a water slide as imagined by Dr. Seuss.

It takes both intuition and full-throttle confidence to start the show with a work this massive and zany. “No one would install the biggest piece in the front; it’s got to be in the back or it’s got to be in the middle,” Marc Glimcher, Pace’s CEO, says during a press preview. “She knew it had to be in the front.”

Past Eve is the vibrant Jewel, a seafoam-tinted confection with layers that curl and collide. Like Eve, its pieces of welded aluminum are shaped by hand, not molds. Signs of human touch have long been important to Shechet, no matter what material she’s using.

Holding court in the back of the room is Flo, the Corten steel work. It’s a wonder of cascading shapes, each a little bit askew: almost-ovals and not-quite triangles and what looks like a pair of legs touching down, ready to dance. Every inch is united in a gorgeous natural patina, streaked like tigereye—a result of the weathering it endured out in the courtyard in Kingston. That’s the beauty of Corten: “It paints itself,” Shechet says.

As with her previous metal pieces at Storm King, she’s taking a traditionally male material (Corten is most associated with Richard Serra) and injecting it with a feminine sensibility, which shows up in the rounded shapes and even in the title. Flo is a nod to Florine Stettheimer, an early-20th-century painter who, explains Shechet, “had freedom that so many women artists didn’t have.”

Shechet, too, has long gone her own way. Born in 1951 in Forest Hills, Queens, she was always interested in putting things together. “I didn’t take sewing. I took woodshop,” she says. She was the only girl in that class.

She studied at New York University for undergrad, then the Rhode Island School of Design (RISD) for grad school. She and her husband raised their two children first in Tribeca, where she had a studio in the basement, and then in Woodstock, where the family moved in 2002.

She taught at RISD and Parsons, juggling work with motherhood and her own practice. It wasn’t easy. “I put up with a lot,” she says. The art world was not always kind to women, much less mothers. “People just wrote you off.”

Over the years her art evolved from smaller clay pieces she could make in her Tribeca basement to larger and more complicated creations with a smattering of materials, including glass, cast iron, and concrete. A 20-year survey of her work in 2015 at the Institute of Contemporary Art in Boston was praised by critics and helped the artist see how her varied materials and approaches are all connected. “I was really struck by how sewn together all these different works were in ways I hadn’t seen before,” she says.

She’s kept busy, chasing her never-ending curiosities with residencies at German porcelain manufacturer Meissen starting in 2012, which led to a revelatory show at The Frick, the first time that museum exhibited a contemporary artist, and the bathroom-fixture giant Kohler in 2017, after which she made her public art debut at Madison Square Park.

In a gallery space adjoinint the front room at Pace are 14 ceramic works, a series titled Touching Ground. It’s the first time Shechet has employed the ancient technique of terra sigillata (“sealed earth” in Latin), in which clay is refined over and over again to make a slip of such fine quality as to be practically waterproof. During the Roman Empire, terra sigillata was used to add a protective sheen to vases and tableware.

There’s poetry in finishing the clay with a layer of its own ultra-refined material, Shechet says. “It’s a very elegant complement.” She was partly inspired by Agnes Martin, who painted in thin layers to achieve her poignant stripes and grids.

The Touching Ground pieces are exercises in juxtaposition: somehow both biomorphic and totally alien, with textures that vary from glossy to sandpaper. The architecture looks mushy. “They’re half falling down, and that’s what I really like,” Shechet says as she shows me a few at her studio back in June. The colors range from beige and bluish gray to hi-vis orange to midnight black, with plenty of airy gaps to keep them from feeling too dense. At Pace, some are ensconced in the walls or cantilevered à la Donald Judd, while others stand atop steel bases.

Often they look like they’ve been sliced down the middle, their guts revealed. “For a ceramicist to successfully expose the inside is kind of against the rules,” Glimcher says, “which is always Arlene’s favorite thing to do: whatever is against the rules.”

Indeed, experimentation is deeply embedded in Shechet’s practice—it always has been. She didn’t need to take on a new material (Corten) or technique (terra sigillata) at this point in her career. But coasting is not in her nature. By staying present in the studio—a Buddhist concept she values greatly—she remains open to whatever bubbles up. That is the through line in all her work.

“I am able to surprise myself,” she says, looking around her studio. “That’s the thing. I have to keep myself interested and surprised.”

“Arlene Shechet: She Said She Said” is on view at Pace Gallery’s 508 and 510 West 25th Street locations through October 24, 2026.
