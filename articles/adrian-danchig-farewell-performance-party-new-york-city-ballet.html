Adrian Danchig-Waring Bid Farewell to New York City Ballet With a Post-Show Rave

To sign off, Danchig-Waring didn’t just curate a matinee program. Rather, fitting for an artist with an expansive sense of the medium’s possibilities, he also gathered his friends for a post-show tropical rave at Performance Space in the East Village.

By Laura Regensdorf

October 20, 2026

How do you measure a storied ballet career? You can look at time spent in the ranks, shaped by rigorous training and its corresponding toll on the body. Or repertoire: the historic roles a dancer has come to define in their own way, along with the new commissions they’ve breathed into being. You can consider the reverberation of influence, both on younger dancers and the company itself. And then there’s an energetic quality, at times radiating light and other times shining it—with or without pulsing strobes—on others.

All of these facets were front of mind on Sunday when the New York City Ballet principal Adrian Danchig-Waring took his final bow with the company, beside a heap of ribbon-tied white roses. Ever since the California native moved into the dorms at the School of American Ballet, its sister institution, in 2001, and joined NYCB two years later, he has been a quietly majestic interpreter of George Balanchine’s canon. Along the way, Danchig-Waring has been equally engaged with today’s dancemakers, both in the studio and in his role as the artistic director of the New York Choreographic Institute.

To sign off, Danchig-Waring didn’t just curate a matinee program with needle-moving classics alongside 21st-century works that reflect his love of process. Rather, fitting for an artist with an expansive sense of the medium’s possibilities, he also gathered his friends for a post-show tropical rave at Performance Space in the East Village. The double whammy meant the usually subdued attire inside the Lincoln Center theater was peppered with disco brights (the comedian-poet Alok Vaid-Menon), buttercream suiting (the playwright Jeremy O. Harris), and Carmen Miranda-esque fruit appliqués (the filmmaker and producer Drew Denny).

“Dancing for me has never been about the solo—it’s about the collective experience of bringing these works to life,” Danchig-Waring said ahead of the show, referring to the four pieces he chose for Sunday’s send-off. The bookends were two of Balanchine’s Black and White ballets—The Four Temperaments (1946) and Agon (1957)—with their pioneering pelvis tilts and flexed wrists, made all the more legible in stripped-back leotards and tights. “These are dances that resonate with me on a cellular level,” he said. “I feel simultaneously at ease inside of their complexity and totally electrified.” He danced the cool-headed Phlegmatic variation in Four Ts and delivered a crystalline pas de deux with Unity Phelan in Agon, to effusive applause. He also singled out two dancers making their debuts in Sunday’s Agon: the soloists Dominika Afanasenkov and David Gabriel. “As I’m stepping off that stage, I want to witness people who are just arriving. I want to roll out the red carpet for them.”
