How to Preserve Your Social Battery

After days full of meetings, appointments, and conversations, our social batteries are sometimes simply depleted. But why do certain encounters exhaust us more than others? A psychologist explains what happens during social exhaustion, how to achieve genuine regeneration, and when withdrawal becomes a warning sign.

By Desireé Oostland

October 20, 2026

You may know the feeling: One event follows another. First, a work function; then a friend’s birthday party that, of course, you don’t want to miss. After all, you don’t want to disappoint anyone. But eventually, even the good things become too much, and your social battery simply runs out. Hamburg-based psychologist Alica Schellhorn explains to Devon what happens when our social energy reserves are depleted—and why some situations leave us completely drained, while others, even those that are considerably busier, barely take any energy at all or may even give us a boost.

What Exactly Is a Social Battery?

We often hear people talk about having a low or empty social battery. But what does that actually mean? When we say our social battery is empty, we usually mean a combination of mental, emotional, and physical exhaustion, Schellhorn explains.

Social interactions require sustained attention, after all: processing social cues, planning responses, and regulating emotions. “Particularly in complex or socially demanding situations, this can lead to an increased subjective sense of strain.” Over time, this can gradually deplete our energy reserves and, so to speak, drain our social battery.

How exhausting social interactions are varies from person to person. “Temperamental traits, such as emotional reactivity, sensitivity to stimuli, and differences in self-regulation, can influence how taxing social situations feel,” the psychologist explains. On average, introverted people find social stimulation less rewarding, while extroverted people are more likely to benefit from social contact.

According to Schellhorn, however, this does not mean that extroverts cannot experience social exhaustion. Other factors also come into play, including the effort required for self-regulation and adapting to others. “For people with social anxiety, heightened self-focused attention can place additional demands on their resources, for example.” People with ADHD or those on the autism spectrum may also experience a high degree of social adaptation, sometimes through a process known as masking or camouflaging.

Situational factors matter, too. A familiar, positive interaction may be far less draining than being in a large, noisy group of unfamiliar people. A lack of sleep and general stress can also reduce the resources available to us.

The Difference Between Feeling Exhausted and Having an Empty Social Battery

Feeling exhausted after a particularly demanding day is perfectly normal, Schellhorn says. To distinguish between ordinary exhaustion and an empty social battery, it is therefore less important to focus on a single symptom than to look at patterns over time.

“If you repeatedly feel exhausted, irritable, or overstimulated after social interactions and then consciously need some quiet time, this may simply reflect a normal need for regulation,” she explains. She adds, “An important indicator is whether taking time to recover actually helps you regain your desire to socialize and engage in other activities.”

The situation is different when withdrawing from others increasingly becomes a way to avoid uncomfortable feelings or social situations. In such cases, Schellhorn advises taking a closer look at what is happening.

What Helps Recharge Your Social Battery?

Just as the causes of social exhaustion vary from person to person, so do the strategies that can help replenish your energy. According to Schellhorn, activities that reduce stimulation and the demands of self-regulation are often effective. These may include getting enough sleep, exercising, spending time in nature, resting, or engaging in activities that do not require you to adapt socially.

“Consciously choosing manageable amounts of social contact with people around whom you feel safe and can be yourself can also be very restorative.”

From a therapeutic perspective, however, it is particularly important to distinguish between recovery and avoidance. “Taking a step back to replenish your resources can be very beneficial.” If, on the other hand, withdrawing primarily serves to prevent you from feeling anxiety, insecurity, or overwhelm in the short term, it can reinforce avoidance behaviors over time.

Schellhorn emphasizes that the goal is not to spend as much or as little time with other people as possible. Rather, it is about finding “a balance between activity, social contact, and recovery that matches your individual capacity.”

How to Properly Recharge When Your Social Battery Is Empty

Research on recovery, including the work of Sabine Sonnentag, suggests that we recharge most effectively when we can mentally switch off, unwind, and decide for ourselves how to spend our time.

Applied to social recovery, this means minimizing stimulation and external expectations. You might go for a walk, ideally in nature, take a bath, read, listen to music, or cook. But there is one thing Schellhorn says we often underestimate: Scrolling through Instagram, checking messages, or browsing other feeds does not provide a genuine break from the social world. We are still constantly exposed to faces, opinions, and comparisons, which keeps our brains in processing mode.

From a therapeutic perspective, Schellhorn often recommends a strategy based on keeping an energy balance sheet. “Spend a week observing which interactions and situations drain your energy and which ones replenish it, then plan accordingly.”

For example, after a long day of meetings, you might decide not to organize a big dinner that evening and instead move it to the weekend. Small transition rituals can also help, such as spending 10 quiet minutes in the car before going home to your family.

“It is important to remember that not every interaction costs energy. Sitting quietly alongside someone you feel comfortable with, each of you reading your own book, can be very restorative.” In other words, taking time out to recharge does not always mean being alone.

How to Set Boundaries Without Feeling Guilty

Friendships can make this particularly challenging. Let’s be honest: Always being the person who cancels because their batteries are running low can become frustrating for both sides over time. At the same time, repeatedly pushing yourself beyond your limits to keep plans is hardly a solution.

So how can you set boundaries or cancel plans without feeling guilty, upsetting others, or jeopardizing relationships that matter to you?

“Setting boundaries does not mean rejecting other people,” Schellhorn says. It means recognizing your own needs and resources, taking appropriate care of them, and communicating them clearly. It helps to be direct, considerate, and timely, without feeling the need to justify yourself at length.

For example, you might say: “I’m realizing that I’m really exhausted right now and need a quiet evening today. I’d love to find another time for us to get together.”

It is also important to remember that “feeling guilty does not automatically mean that we have done something wrong.” People who are highly attentive to others’ needs may feel guilty when setting boundaries. That feeling is allowed to be there, but it does not mean you have to abandon your boundaries to make it go away.

From a psychological perspective, this can be explained by learned behaviors and social expectations. “If we learn early in life to maintain harmony, meet expectations, or prioritize other people’s needs, setting a boundary can initially feel wrong or selfish,” Schellhorn explains.

The resulting guilt is often less an objective indication that we have done something wrong than a signal that we are acting against an expectation we have learned to follow. “This is a learning process for our nervous system.”

When an Empty Social Battery Becomes a Warning Sign

A strong need for solitude is not automatically a problem. What matters is understanding the purpose of withdrawing from others and how it affects your life.

“You should take a closer look if your withdrawal increases over an extended period, you begin giving up social contacts or other important activities, and you find yourself experiencing little joy or interest in things that used to matter to you.”

According to Schellhorn, consistently avoiding social situations out of fear, shame, or a feeling of not being good enough can also indicate psychological distress. “In these cases, depressive symptoms, social anxiety, or prolonged overwhelm may be contributing factors.”

As a general guideline, if this state persists for more than two weeks, it is worth taking seriously. “In these cases, it can help to speak with your primary care physician or schedule an initial psychotherapy consultation. The earlier you address what is happening, the easier it is generally to make a change.”
