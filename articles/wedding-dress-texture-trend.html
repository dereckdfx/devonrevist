Unexpected Textures Were the Breakout Trend From Bridal Fashion Week

Unexpected texture is one of biggest wedding dress trends out of New York Bridal Fashion Week fall 2027 collections. Designers share the inspiration behind their unique pieces.

By Shelby Wax, Corinne Pierre-Louis

October 22, 2026

Texture is no stranger to a bridal salon—but new takes on traditional, tactile materials are coming. Wedding dresses have followed a similar formula for the last few decades, using dynamic lace patterns, rhinestone embellishments, sumptuous silks, and voluminous tulle to bring visual interest to the couture-level gowns brides wear as they exchange vows. And by no means are these materials going anywhere. Yet when we previewed new collections for fall 2027 at New York Bridal Fashion Week, we were surprised to find so many unfamiliar appliqués and fabrics that we were constantly asking the models if we could touch their outfits.

While we are seeing a trend of more traditional bridal silhouettes in recent seasons, designers are also finding ways to reinvent the wheel in a market that can often feel repetitive. From choosing unique adornments to manipulating fabric into new material states to dynamic weaving and placement of more traditional toiles, these pieces feel creative, boundary-breaking, and perfect for the bride who wants to wear a true piece of art on their wedding day.

Ahead, we asked bridal designers with show-stopping, textural wedding dresses this season to break down the inspiration and technical processes behind their creations.

Lein

“With the Daniela Dress, I wanted to explore the relationship between simplicity and ornamentation,” says Lein founder and designer Meredith Stoecklein. “The silhouette is intentionally simple and relaxed, allowing the texture and movement of the embellishment to become the focus. Inspired by the irregular surfaces and organic formations found in nature, silver faceted beads are worked into layers of delicate tulle and bobbinet, creating a texture that feels almost mineral-like.”

“I loved the idea of taking something traditionally precious and giving it a raw, imperfect quality,” she continues. “The beading follows the lines of the body, framing the draped open back and extending beyond the hem, creating movement and an unexpected sense of fluidity against the structure of the crepe.”

Marmar Halim

“This gown was inspired by my muse: the creative girl. With this collection, I wanted to capture the playful, glamorous spirit of the 1920s, particularly the beauty and movement of feathers, but reinterpret it in a modern and unexpected way,” says designer Marmar Halim. “This is one of my most creative pieces and one I truly loved making. Rather than using traditional feathers, I wanted to explore how fabric itself could recreate that same sense of movement and texture.”

“We started by manipulating brocade, cutting it into delicate strips and draping each piece directly onto the mannequin to follow and define the woman’s body. Every detail was then hand-stitched, with fine wires incorporated into the lower part of the gown to create movement and sculptural volume, blending with soft layers of tulle,” she explains. “For me, this dress represents the freedom to experiment. I wanted to challenge the traditional idea of bridal wear by transforming something classic into something unexpected, while still preserving the femininity, lightness, and glamour of the 1920s.”

Kyha

“The guiding principle in developing and sourcing fabrications for Josephine was capturing the textures and movement we encountered across the Balearic Islands,” says Kyha Scott, founder and designer of Kyha. “This particular fabrication was inspired by the wild grasses that characterize the island landscape. Its texture references this native flora's movement and energy on the islands.”

“Metallic threads are woven throughout the fabric, catching and reflecting light in a way that connects back to the beading we developed for this collection,” she explains. “When we began draping the fabric, we discovered its natural tendency to fall into sculptural folds. Rather than working against this, we allowed the fabrication to inform the silhouette. The result is a piece that celebrates the interplay of texture, movement, and structure, bringing together the natural influences of the Balearic landscape with precision and architectural form.”

Mia Atelier

“We used a cotton blend in a clay-like colour to echo the calico toile, the working prototype of a gown,” explains Mia Chen, founder and creative director of Mia Atelier. “The fabric holds structure beautifully and has a matte finish, with a raw, tactile quality reminiscent of an artist’s canvas.”

“The inspiration came from what happens behind the lens: the fittings, the workroom, and the dress taking shape,” she elaborates. “There is so much beauty in the process of dressmaking that people rarely get to see, and I wanted to bring that into the finished piece. It was about preserving the spirit of the toile while shaping it into a finished bridal look. The rawness and sense of being slightly undone are deliberate.” The designer adds, “It may feel imperfect, but the decision is intentional and considered.”

Floure Studio

“Ostria was born from my fascination with the female form and the idea of creating texture through structure rather than embellishment,” explains designer Flourenchia Tamir, designer and founder of Floure Studio. “I wanted the bodice to feel almost like a piece of sculpture, with an intricate woven surface that follows and accentuates the natural curves of the body. The process was particularly intricate, as each interlacing detail on the bodice had to be carefully constructed by weaving hundreds of meters of rouleau and while maintaining the tension and precision of the corsetry underneath. The sculpted, spiral-like bust detailing introduces a softer, more organic contrast to the geometric weave.”

“The back of the gown unfolds into three oversized, abstract rosette drapings, sculpted from generous volumes of silk,” she continues. “Rather than creating traditional, perfectly formed flowers, I wanted them to feel spontaneous and almost undone, with layers of fabric twisting, gathering and falling naturally into the dramatic train. Each rosette brings its own dimension and movement, transforming the skirt into something almost like a living sculpture.” Says Tamir, “For me, Ostria is about the tension between structure and fluidity. I wanted the gown to feel both architectural and romantic, celebrating femininity in all its complexity.”
