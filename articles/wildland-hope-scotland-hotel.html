This Stylish New Scottish Highlands Retreat Is a Love Letter to Its Natural Surroundings

As Europe’s largest private conservation project, WildLand is as much about impeccable design as it is about protecting the local flora and fauna.

By Hayley Maitland

August 31, 2026

Like so many quirks of British culture, Highlands tourism owes a debt to the royals. In the dead of winter 1848, Queen Victoria and Prince Albert, taken with the “wild loveliness” of Aberdeenshire, signed a lease on Balmoral, and suddenly the furthest reaches of Bonnie Scotland—or the tartan-draped version of them depicted in Sir Walter Scott’s Waverley—were in fashion with the Debrett’s set. Bagpipes were trotted out, ceilidhs given, drams of whisky poured, and, rather more permanently, heathered swaths of Caledonia repurposed as sporting estates. By the 20th century, summer excursions to the Highlands were a fixture of the aristocratic calendar, with transatlantic visitors soon joining the English to shoot, fish, and feign indifference to horizontal rain. “The charm of Scotland in August and September draws an influx of sportsmen from all over the world,” Devon reported in 1930. “For two months, she is invaded…and herself left entirely unmoved by so much frenzied activity.”

Except, of course, that wasn’t entirely true; already, by then, Scotland’s fragile landscape was under threat from ecological degradation. Nearly a century on, it ranks among the most nature-depleted countries in the world, with land mismanagement across grouse moors and deer parks a contributing factor. As one laird I speak to puts it: “That Romantic vision that everyone has from Landseer paintings—the endless heaths, the rolling glens—really, that’s a biodiversity disaster captured in real time.”

It’s a crisis WildLand is hoping to remedy with a 200-year vision. Established in 2006 by Danish businessman Anders Holch Povlsen—the largest private landowner in Scotland—the conservation group’s holdings now encompass more than 222,000 acres, with a hospitality arm that immerses guests in rewilded landscapes. (More than six million trees have been hand-planted by Wildland in the last 12 years alone.) Aldourie Castle, a turreted pile on the shores of Loch Ness, and Killiehuntly Farmhouse, a slate-roofed retreat nestled in the granite peaks of the Cairngorms, are among its flagship properties—but its latest opening on the northwest coast might pip them both to become the standout.

Available for hotel stays from August to October and for private hire the rest of the year, Hope started life as a shooting lodge for the 3rd Duke of Sutherland in 1867—standing between the cold, green waters of the North Atlantic and a loch whose pebbled shores are still dotted with the abandoned cottages of farmers displaced by the Highland Clearances. Rising vertiginously above them: the 3,000-foot peak of Ben Hope, which is haloed with mist when I pull off the North Coast 500 and down Hope’s driveway one pearly-skied day in May.

A sudden downpour only makes the Scots-Baronial lodge feel all the more welcoming; birch logs spit in Victorian grates, pots of loose-leaf tea have been laid out beside homemade shortbread in The Drawing Room, and classic novels from Chelsea’s John Sandoe Books are piled in willow baskets. Hands around a mug of Earl Grey, I’m led up to my room, one of just seven in the property, each as cozy as an Aran knit: picture antique four-posters draped with hand-dyed linens; a color palette borrowed from bell heather and limestone crags; and freestanding baths whose caddies are set with linden-scented salts. On cold nights, the turndown service at Hope includes hot-water bottles slipped between the Aberdeenshire-made mattresses and duvets, with flasks of herbal teas occasionally left by the bed.

The 3rd Duke might well be scandalized by how convivial Hope is these days. There are no formalities here, with guests encouraged to help themselves to sticky flapjacks from The Butler’s Pantry and single malts in The Billiards Room whenever the mood strikes, while the WildLand team are more like gracious hosts who just happen to have environmental-science degrees, chatting knowledgeably about everything from peatland restoration to wildlife corridors. Still, as relaxed as the mood may appear, there are grande-dame hotels that could take notes from the service, with the team remembering everything from my Wellington-boot size to the fact that I’d admired a particular vase of tulips on my first afternoon. The exact variety, I’m thoughtfully informed before leaving, was a Silver Cloud brought in from the walled gardens at Aldourie Castle, where many of Hope’s fruit and vegetables come from, too.

As I learn over a dinner of roast chicken, wild greens, and foraged morels on my first night, it’s Cécile & Boyd—the design firm behind Singita’s fabled safari lodges—that oversaw Hope’s decade-long renovation. In keeping with WildLand’s conservation philosophy, the team restored as much of the original lodge as possible: reinforcing the ironwork of the façade, leaving the burlap wall-coverings in place, and retaining the wooden benches set into the Victorian porch, where tweed-clad guests once stopped to pull off their waders after casting for brown trout.

Nearly a third of the furniture was in place before the refurb, too—much of it brought over from the Sutherland’s principal seat of Dunrobin Castle long ago—with newer additions echoing the style of the original pieces, from Jacobean oak writing desks to a collection of Georgian silver salt cellars. Contemporary art and design also line the communal rooms, from leather-wrapped stones sourced via Edinburgh’s Bard to watercolor landscapes by Scottish painter Victoria Crowe, but really it’s the natural world that takes center stage at Hope, even when you’re indoors.

Downstairs in The Den, glass walls frame the Caledonian pines outside, while a foraged-heather chandelier by The New Craftsmen hangs over the dining table. As for the native flora taking root throughout the sloping gardens: Tom Stuart-Smith, the landscape designer behind the grounds at Windsor Castle, is responsible for the romantic planting. From my room, “Solas”—named after the Gaelic word for light—I spy climbing hydrangeas and lily-of-the-valley, palest foxgloves and guelder-rose, Loch Hope gleaming beyond them.

It’s through these gardens—and over a plashing burn—that you find The Clachlan, a purpose-built cluster of stone buildings constructed by Edinburgh-based firm Gras, and the jumping-off point for all country pursuits at Hope. After a conservation briefing with WildLand’s field guides each morning, guests are invited to choose their own adventure in The Steadings, a cavernous space filled with outdoor gear ranging from Simpel wetsuits to Le Chameau boots, Argo ATVs to Spot Gen4 phones. Activities run the gamut from paddleboarding off the coast of Rabbit Island to 4x4 excursions around the 100,000-acre estate, trekking with Shetland ponies to sand yachting across Tongue Bay. It’s most enlightening, though, to follow one of Hope’s suggested itineraries, each of which is based around a specific element (fire, earth) or feeling (grounding, stillness), with nods to WildLand’s rewilding successes gently woven in throughout.

A day themed around water begins with a cold plunge in the loch, closely followed by a mad dash into the wood-fired sauna in the lodge grounds —then it’s off to clamber up the mossy, bracken-strewn side of Ben Hope, the guides stopping every so often to point out the willow, larch and juniper trees beginning to flourish on its slopes again, or to call my attention to the song of a cuckoo (a rare sound elsewhere in Britain now, where populations have dropped significantly). Less than an hour later, the path brings us out beneath a 100-foot waterfall, where a picnic of vegetable soup and still-warm bread is laid on a tartan blanket, before I take a nap in a bed of heather. Even back at the lodge, the dinner menu is a tribute to the waters of the Scottish coast: langoustines grilled over a firepit; cured monkfish with sea lettuces and wild horseradish; and young potatoes boiled with seaweed foraged from a nearby cove.

On my final morning, I decide to visit the latter, following the primrose-dotted banks of the River Hope until it brings me out at a rushing estuary. At one end I find the ruins of a Victorian ice-house, once used by 19th-century fishermen to store the day’s catch, while at the other, a crumbling bothy stands above the aquamarine water, framed by bright gorse. Hope is currently restoring them both with its usual meticulousness—and working to keep the beach in its pristine state. Harbor porpoises are still a common sight offshore here, diving among the kelp forests, while tidal pools cradle anemones and vibrant starfish, and yet there’s not a single footprint on the golden sand, the only noise the cries of the seabirds whirling overhead.

It would almost be too picturesque a scene, if there weren’t lead-gray clouds gathering in the distance. “All that is needed for the complete enjoyment of Scotland,” Devon advised readers in 1930, “is a waterproof.” It wasn’t wrong—but a reservation at Hope certainly doesn’t hurt.
